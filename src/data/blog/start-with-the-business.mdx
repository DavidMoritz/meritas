<blockquote>“Be curious, not judgemental.”</blockquote>
<cite>-- Walt Whitman</cite>

As a software engineer who consults with corporations on enterprise app development, I've learned one fundamental rule that stands above all others: **Start with the Business**. This isn't just a guideline; it's a cornerstone for creating software that not only functions but thrives within the ecosystem of the company it's designed for.

## Understanding the Business Core

Before you even begin to sketch out a user interface or write a single line of code, you must immerse yourself in the business you're serving. Here's why:

1. **Context is King**: Every business operates within its unique context - its market, competitors, customers, and internal processes. Without understanding these elements, designing an app is like navigating a city without a map. You might get somewhere, but it won't be where you need to go.
2. **Deep Insight Over Surface Features**: Many developers jump straight to the "what" - what features should the app have? However, the real value comes from asking "why". Why does this feature matter? How does it align with business goals? Understanding the 'why' allows you to design solutions that are not just feature-rich but strategically impactful.
3. **Enhancing Business Value**: When your curiosity extends beyond the superficial layer of app functionality to the core of the business operations, you can identify opportunities for automation, efficiency, or even new revenue streams that might not have been obvious at first glance. This is where you shift from being a code writer to a strategic partner.

## Practical Steps to Understand the Business
- **Engage in Deep Dialogues**: Spend time talking to various stakeholders - executives, department heads, frontline employees. Each will give you a piece of the puzzle. Ask about their daily challenges, the bottlenecks in their workflows, and the dreams they have for their department or the company.
- **Shadowing and Observation**: Sometimes, the most profound insights come from simply watching how things are done. Spend a day or more shadowing different roles to see the software ecosystem in action.
- **Document Business Processes**: Map out the current workflows. This not only helps in understanding but also in spotting inefficiencies that your software can address.
- **Feedback Loops**: Establish continuous feedback mechanisms. Whether it's through formal meetings or informal chats, keep the lines of communication open. Businesses evolve, and your software should evolve with them.

## Note on User/Customer Pain Points

A crucial part of understanding the business is identifying where users or customers face friction in their interactions with existing systems or processes. These pain points are often where your software can make the most significant impact. Whether it's a cumbersome checkout process, slow data retrieval, or a lack of intuitive navigation, recognizing these issues is key to creating a solution that truly resonates with its users.

## User Testing and Journey Maps

Developing user testing sessions and creating detailed journey maps can illuminate these pain points. User testing involves observing real users as they interact with your product or prototype, providing direct feedback on what works and what doesn't. Journey maps, on the other hand, visualize the entire experience from a user's perspective, from initial contact to post-purchase support. This process not only helps in identifying where users struggle but also in understanding their motivations, emotions, and the points of delight or frustration. By addressing these pain points, you ensure that your software not only meets but exceeds user expectations, enhancing satisfaction and engagement.

## The Impact of Curiosity Over Judgment
Walt Whitman's advice to be curious rather than judgmental is particularly poignant in software development. It's easy to judge a business's current state or the inefficiencies in their old systems. However, judgment leads to solutions that might fit a preconceived notion rather than the real-world scenario. Curiosity, on the other hand, opens up pathways to innovation by understanding and embracing the complexities of the business.

In my consulting experience, every successful project has one thing in common: a thorough, empathetic understanding of the business before any coding begins. This approach doesn't just build apps; it builds solutions that drive business forward. Remember, if your curiosity doesn’t extend beyond the surface, the value you provide will be a mere fraction of what it could be. So, start with the business, delve deep, and let your software reflect not just the needs but the vision of the enterprise you're serving.
